## Sharjah’s Infrastructure Boost: Water, Ports, and Waste Power a New Growth Cycle  
#WaterSecurity #EnergyTransition

Sharjah is advancing a suite of infrastructure projects designed to secure its economic future and resource resilience. By 2028, Sharjah’s first Independent Water Project, developed by SEWA and ACWA Power, will produce 410,000 cubic meters of desalinated water daily using seawater reverse osmosis technology. In parallel, the Sharjah Ports Authority extended its concession with Gulftainer by 35 years for operations at Sharjah Container Terminal and Khorfakkan Container Terminal, underpinning logistics capacity. Additionally, the waste-to-energy facility operated jointly by BEEAH and Tadweer Group is doubling its annual waste processing capacity from 300,000 to 600,000 tonnes and increasing power output from 30 MW to 60 MW.

These projects carry significant operational and commercial implications. The water project enhances Sharjah’s water security independently of federal supplies, critical for urban and industrial demand. The long-term port concession extension ensures continuity and potential expansion of trade throughput, especially with the planned Al Dhaid Multi-Modal Trade Corridor linking Khorfakkan Port to Etihad Rail and targeting 1.5 million TEUs annually. The waste-to-energy expansion not only improves waste management but also contributes to Sharjah’s renewable energy generation and net zero targets, leveraging a $220 million initial investment and joint venture financing.

Regionally, Sharjah’s integrated approach reflects broader UAE priorities to diversify infrastructure models—combining independent utility projects, long-term concessions, and joint ventures—to build resilient, sustainable urban ecosystems. This positions Sharjah as a logistics and industrial hub capable of supporting the UAE’s economic diversification while addressing resource constraints and environmental goals.

SOMA’s Perspective: Sharjah’s coordinated infrastructure investments illustrate a strategic layering of resource security, logistics capacity, and clean energy generation that goes beyond isolated projects. The mix of financing and partnership models mitigates risks and aligns operational incentives across sectors, which is critical in a region balancing rapid growth with sustainability mandates. Investors and operators should watch how these projects integrate with federal initiatives and regional trade corridors, as Sharjah’s model could set benchmarks for scaling resilient infrastructure in the Gulf.

## Egypt’s New Lithium-Ion Battery Plant: Localizing Energy Storage for a Renewables Future  
#EnergyTransition #Manufacturing

Egypt is moving to strengthen its energy transition infrastructure with a new lithium-ion battery assembly facility developed by local firm Teletech and Russia’s Rosatom energy storage unit. The plant will have an annual production capacity of 500 MWh and target sectors including power generation, telecommunications, data centers, oil and gas, and other industrial applications. This initiative aims to reduce Egypt’s reliance on imported battery technologies and external supply chains, while building domestic technical expertise.

Operationally, the facility will enhance Egypt’s ability to support grid stability amid rising renewable energy integration, a critical factor as the country expands solar and wind capacity. By localizing battery assembly, Egypt can shorten supply chains, reduce costs, and improve responsiveness to sectoral needs such as telecom backup power and industrial energy storage. The project also signals growing industrial diversification aligned with national energy policies.

In the regional context, Egypt’s battery assembly plant reflects a broader MENA trend toward developing local manufacturing capabilities for clean energy technologies. This move strengthens supply chain resilience in a geopolitically sensitive region and positions Egypt as a hub for energy storage solutions supporting both domestic and export markets.

SOMA’s Perspective: The battery plant marks a strategic pivot from import dependency to local capability building in energy storage—a sector crucial for managing intermittency in renewables-heavy grids. For investors and policymakers, this development underscores the importance of integrating manufacturing with energy transition goals to create durable value chains. Egypt’s approach may inspire similar initiatives in neighboring markets seeking to balance rapid renewable deployment with industrial growth.

## Iraq Eyes Solar Expansion with Saudi Acwa: Regional Collaboration in Renewables  
#EnergyTransition #RenewableEnergy

Iraq is currently in negotiations with Saudi Arabia’s Acwa Power to increase its solar energy capacity. While detailed terms and project specifics remain undisclosed, this engagement signals Iraq’s intent to accelerate renewable energy development through partnerships with established regional players. Acwa Power’s experience across MENA’s solar markets positions it as a strategic partner for Iraq’s energy diversification efforts.

If realized, this collaboration could bring operational and investment momentum to Iraq’s nascent solar sector, potentially unlocking financing, technology transfer, and project management expertise. Expanding solar capacity aligns with Iraq’s broader energy transition objectives and the region’s net zero ambitions, offering a pathway to reduce fossil fuel dependence and improve energy security.

Regionally, the talks exemplify growing cross-border cooperation in renewables, reflecting how Gulf-based developers are extending their footprint into emerging markets. This dynamic may accelerate renewable capacity additions across MENA, but also raises questions about regulatory frameworks, grid integration, and local capacity building in Iraq.

SOMA’s Perspective: Iraq’s negotiations with Acwa highlight a critical phase where regional partnerships can bridge gaps in capital, technology, and expertise for renewable projects. However, the success of such deals will depend on Iraq’s ability to create enabling policies and infrastructure to absorb and sustain new solar capacity. For investors, this underscores the importance of assessing not just project potential but also the broader ecosystem readiness in emerging MENA markets.
