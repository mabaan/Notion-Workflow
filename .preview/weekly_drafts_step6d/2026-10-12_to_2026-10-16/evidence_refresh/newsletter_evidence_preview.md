## Sharjah’s Infrastructure Push: Water, Waste, and Ports Powering the Next Growth Cycle  
#WaterSecurity #EnergyTransition

Sharjah is set to boost its infrastructure with a landmark independent water project delivering 410,000 cubic metres of desalinated water per day by 2028. Utilizing seawater reverse osmosis technology, this facility will serve around 1.4 million residents, addressing critical water security needs. Alongside this, Sharjah Ports Authority secured a 35-year extension of its concession with Gulftainer in 2023, ensuring continuity in port operations and logistics capacity.

The emirate is also doubling its waste-to-energy processing capacity from 300,000 to 600,000 tonnes annually, with power generation capacity rising from 30 MW to 60 MW. This expansion, a public-private partnership between BEEAH and Tadweer Group backed by a $220 million financial close, reflects Sharjah’s commitment to sustainable waste management and clean energy production. Additionally, the planned Al Dhaid Multi-Modal Trade Corridor aims to integrate Khorfakkan Port with Etihad Rail, targeting an annual capacity of 1.5 million TEUs, further strengthening logistics infrastructure.

These coordinated investments address Sharjah’s long-term resilience by securing vital water supplies, enhancing waste-to-energy output, and expanding port and rail connectivity. This integrated approach supports the emirate’s economic diversification while aligning with the UAE’s broader net zero and water security ambitions.

SOMA’s Perspective: Sharjah’s strategy highlights the trade-offs between scaling essential utilities and maintaining sustainability. Observers should monitor how the desalination project balances energy demand with water output, and how the expanded waste-to-energy facility integrates into the local grid. The extended port concession also raises questions about evolving public-private governance models in critical infrastructure sectors.

## Egypt’s New Lithium-Ion Battery Plant: Localizing Clean Energy Supply Chains  
#EnergyTransition #Manufacturing

Egypt’s Teletech and Russia’s Rosatom have signed an agreement to establish a lithium-ion battery assembly facility with an annual production capacity of 500 MWh. This plant will serve multiple sectors, including power generation, telecommunications, data centers, oil and gas, and other industrial applications. The project aims to reduce Egypt’s reliance on imported battery technologies and external supply chains.

By building local manufacturing capabilities, the facility supports Egypt’s energy transition goals and enhances grid stability amid increasing renewable energy integration. It also fosters the development of domestic technical expertise, which is essential for sustaining long-term energy infrastructure growth. The plant’s diverse sectoral focus underscores the strategic importance of batteries beyond electricity storage, extending into critical industrial and telecom systems.

This development reflects Egypt’s broader push to strengthen energy security and resilience through localized production. It also positions the country as a regional hub for clean energy components, potentially influencing supply chain dynamics across North Africa and the Middle East.

SOMA’s Perspective: The battery assembly plant presents a material opportunity to reduce supply chain vulnerabilities but also introduces challenges in scaling production quality and cost competitiveness. Stakeholders should watch how Egypt navigates technology transfer, workforce development, and integration with renewable projects to maximize the facility’s impact.

## Iraq’s Solar Ambitions: Negotiations with Saudi Acwa Signal Regional Energy Collaboration  
#EnergyTransition #RenewableEnergy

Iraq is currently in discussions with Saudi Arabia’s Acwa to expand its solar energy capacity. While details remain limited, these negotiations indicate a strategic intent to accelerate renewable energy deployment in Iraq. Acwa’s involvement suggests potential access to established project development expertise and financing channels from the Gulf region.

Increasing solar capacity aligns with Iraq’s need to diversify its energy mix and reduce reliance on fossil fuels. It also fits within broader MENA trends where cross-border partnerships are instrumental in scaling renewables. Given Iraq’s energy demand growth and infrastructure challenges, such cooperation could facilitate faster project delivery and technology transfer.

However, the talks also underscore the complexities of regional energy collaboration, including geopolitical considerations and regulatory frameworks. The outcome will be a bellwether for how Iraq leverages Gulf partnerships to meet its net zero and energy security objectives.

SOMA’s Perspective: Iraq’s negotiations with Acwa highlight the balancing act between ambition and execution risk in renewable expansion. External stakeholders should focus on the negotiation’s progress, financing structures, and regulatory alignment to assess the viability and scale of upcoming solar projects.
